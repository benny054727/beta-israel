**Beta Israel** ("House of Israel") is the name the Jewish community of Ethiopia used for itself. For centuries, community members lived mainly in northern Ethiopia: around Gondar, in the Simien Mountains, near Lake Tana and in the Tigray region. They lived in small villages, usually close to a river or spring, as the laws of ritual purity required.

## Traditions about their origins

Several traditions, within the community and beyond, describe where Ethiopian Jews came from:

- **The tribe of Dan:** according to this tradition, Beta Israel are descendants of the tribe of Dan, one of the ten tribes. Rabbi Ovadia Yosef relied on this tradition in his 1973 halachic ruling recognizing them as Jews.
- **The Queen of Sheba and King Solomon:** an ancient Ethiopian tradition tells of Menelik, son of the Queen of Sheba and King Solomon, and of Israelites who came to Ethiopia with him.
- **Jews who came by way of Egypt:** another tradition tells of Jews who traveled from the Land of Israel to Ethiopia through Egypt during the First or Second Temple period.

## A life of preserving identity

For generations, the community held on to its faith while living apart from the rest of the Jewish world. They kept the commandments as written in the **Orit**, the Torah in the Ge'ez language, observed the Sabbath and the purity laws with great care, and carried prayers and longing for Jerusalem.

Many community members were craftspeople: blacksmiths, potters, weavers and builders. They were often not allowed to own land. Their neighbors called them **"Falasha"**, meaning "strangers" or "wanderers". The term is considered offensive, and the community prefers the name **Beta Israel**.

> "Jerusalem" was not just a place on a map: it was a dream passed down from generation to generation, in prayer and in song.
